Mine a share.
Sell it for bitcoin.

Every share you mine carries an output on the share chain, and it is yours to sell. Market makers buy those outputs for bitcoin, over Lightning or over Ark, and take a coinbase slot in your place. A coinbase has room for only so many miners, and that ceiling stopped the original P2Pool from growing. The market is how this pool pays past it. No operator keeps the books, and your money goes straight to a key you hold.

Whoever pays you decides what you mine

DATUM and Stratum V2 let a miner build their own block template, and that is real progress. But both leave the share accounting on a server the pool operates, and that server decides what goes into the payout distribution it hands back to you.

Your freedom to choose transactions is therefore worth exactly what the pool will pay you for using it. A pool operator is a company in a jurisdiction, and companies can be leaned on. A company told which transactions it may not help confirm need not reject your template, or argue with you, or announce anything at all. It stops counting your shares.

DATUM and Stratum V2 leave that lever in place. The accounting is the lever, so the accounting is where the pressure lands.

P2Poolv2 moves the accounting to every node. Each node replicates the share chain, recomputes the PPLNS window, and checks the coinbase your miner works on. Drop a miner from the payout and every other node sees a chain that does not add up.

Governments are good at cutting off the heads of centrally controlled networks like Napster, but pure P2P networks like Gnutella and Tor seem to be holding their own.

What we changed since the first P2Pool

The original P2Pool proved the idea and then ran into three walls: orphaned shares at scale, hashrate readings that made miners think it was broken, and a coinbase that could only hold so many outputs. Each has a specific answer here.

A graph, not a line

Shares reference up to three uncles, so work that arrives late or races another share still gets paid instead of being orphaned. An uncle is paid with a 10% discount, the share that references it earns a 10% bonus, and each uncle can be claimed once. More miners fit in the chain, and the pool’s variance drops as they join.

Vardiff for each miner

P2Pool handed every rig the whole pool’s difficulty, which sampled far too slowly to report a small miner’s hashrate honestly. Our Stratum v1 server, written from scratch in Rust, runs per-miner vardiff at a three-second target, the same as CKPool. Your local dashboard shows your rigs; a separate dashboard shows the whole chain.

Shares that carry Script

The share chain has its own UTXOs and its own Script, built on rust-bitcoin. Every share has a share-chain coinbase paying one output to an address you control. You sell that output, and because it is a real UTXO under a real script, the sale settles atomically instead of on a promise.

Compact blocks on the wire

A share is a compact block of roughly 30 kB rather than a full block. At five shares every ten seconds, a node relays the chain on about 1.3 Mbps instead of 176 Mbps. Home connections can carry a full node.

  • Run your own node, or use someone else’s. A private node in front of your own farm keeps your work and your payouts under your roof. A public node lets other miners point hashrate at you.
  • You get paid one of two ways. If your share of the work puts you among the top N miners for a block, your payout is an output in that block’s coinbase. If it does not, and for most miners it will not, you sell your share-chain outputs to a market maker for bitcoin instead. Either way the payment goes straight to a key you hold.
  • Bring two addresses. Your bitcoin address is where a coinbase payout lands. Your P2Poolv2 address owns the share-chain coinbase, which is the asset you sell. Neither is derived from the other, and the share address must be spend-capable, because the share chain runs real script validation.
  • Set it from your miner’s password field. Put p2p=<your p2pool address> first in the password field so firmware that truncates long passwords still leaves a valid address. Running your own node instead? Set it once in config.toml and leave the rigs alone.
  • Watch it in Grafana. The node exposes Prometheus metrics at /metrics, with dashboards for per-rig hashrate, chain-wide hashrate by miner, effort into the current block, and coinbase distribution.

How a share becomes bitcoin

Payout scalability is the problem this solves. Bitmain’s firmware caps how many outputs a coinbase transaction may carry, which capped how many miners the original P2Pool could pay, and so capped the variance it could absorb. Paying every miner from the coinbase does not scale. Paying a handful of buyers does.

So P2Poolv2 pays the top N miners from the coinbase and gives every other miner a share-chain output instead. Market makers buy those outputs for bitcoin and take the coinbase slot the seller would have had. Ten to fifty buyers can settle a pool of any size, so miner count stops depending on transaction size.

The trade is an HTLC on the share chain against a payment on bitcoin, both locked to the same payment hash. Paying reveals the preimage, and the preimage releases the shares. The two halves succeed together or fail together, so neither side need trust the other.

That bitcoin half can be a Lightning invoice or an Ark VHTLC. Both settle in seconds and both carry the same hash lock, so the share chain sees the same swap either way. Ark suits a small miner better. Receiving over Lightning means holding inbound liquidity, and a home miner who sells a few shares a fortnight has no reason to keep a channel funded. Adaptor signatures remain a third option.

  1. Alice sets the lock

    The miner picks a 32-byte preimage and asks to be paid under its hash: a BOLT11 or BOLT12 invoice on Lightning, or a VHTLC on Ark.

  2. Alice locks her shares

    She locks her share-chain outputs in an HTLC under the same payment hash, so only the preimage releases them.

  3. Bob checks both sides

    The market maker confirms the share HTLC is final and that the hash in it matches the hash he is being asked to pay under.

  4. Bob pays

    Settling on either rail reveals the preimage to him. That is the whole trick: paying is what unlocks the shares.

  5. Bob claims the shares

    He spends the HTLC on the share chain with the preimage. If he never pays, Alice refunds herself after the timeout, or they both sign an instant refund.

The trading window

A share-chain coinbase matures after about a day of share blocks, for the reason bitcoin makes you wait: it stops a miner selling shares and then reorganising them away. The window closes when the PPLNS window moves past that depth, because the share stops counting toward the next payout. Between those two edges the output is tradable, which makes it a short-dated hashrate contract.

Run a node

You need a bitcoin node the P2Poolv2 node can talk to, and Rust. Use the docker signet if you would rather not point anything real at it yet. 

# build and run against your own bitcoind
$ git clone https://github.com/p2poolv2/p2poolv2
$ cd p2poolv2
$ just run

# or bring up a local signet with bitcoind and a miner
$ cd docker && docker compose build
$ ./run-p2pool.sh signet bitcoind
# point a rig at the node
url      stratum+tcp://<your-node>:3333
user     <your bitcoin address>
password p2p=<your p2pool address>

Share addresses are bech32m: p2pool1 on mainnet, tp2pool1 on testnet4, and sp2pool1 on signet.
